# Dependency Triad: A Metric to Quantify the Dependencies Between Attributes for Local Differential Privacy 

Sandaru Jayawardana ∗, Sennur Ulukus †, Ming Ding ‡and Kanchana Thilakarathna ∗∗The University of Sydney, Australia 

†University of Maryland, USA 

‡ Technology, CSIRO, Australia 

Abstract —Collecting multidimensional user data is essential for extracting rich insights across various applications. Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has emerged as a de facto standard for mitigating privacy risks in such scenarios. A key challenge in privacy-preserving multidimensional data collection lies in inter-attribute dependencies, as they can inadvertently reveal corre-lated information and increase privacy vulnerabilities. Therefore, accurately measuring correlation-induced privacy leakage (CPL) is essential for privacy analysis and privacy-utility trade-off. However, existing CPL analysis solutions either require accurate prior knowledge or face scalability challenges for large numbers of attributes and high-cardinality attributes. These limit their practical applicability in real data. To address this research gap, we propose a novel metric, “ Dependency Triad ” (DT), which sum-marizes the pairwise dependency information relevant to CPL using three parameters and yields a constant-time conservative estimator of pairwise CPL. DT explicitly models uncertainty in prior distributional knowledge through its parameters, delivering robust leakage estimates. Moreover, its robustness to sparse distributions makes it particularly suitable for high-cardinality attributes, while the pairwise formulation serves as a tractable building block for assessing total leakage in multidimensional set-tings. Extensive experiments on both synthetic and real datasets demonstrate that DT consistently estimates CPL across diverse dependency regimes and prior uncertainties. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In today’s information age, collecting multidimensional 

user records (i.e., records which contain multiple fea-tures/attributes) is crucial for applications such as personalized services [1], [2] and data crowd-sourcing [3], [4]. These records often include sensitive attributes— personally identi-fiable information (PII) [5], financial [6], and health [7]— creating substantial privacy risks. ε-local differential privacy (ε-LDP) [8] is the de facto standard to provide privacy when curators are untrusted, or users will not share raw data [9]. An LDP mechanism is a randomized algorithm that perturbs data to bound worst-case leakage by ε (≥ 0). Here, a smaller 

ε yields stronger privacy (more noise) but typically results in lower data utility (data accuracy); therefore, experts tune ε

to trade off between data utility and privacy. LDP has been deployed at scale by industry leaders such as Google [1], Microsoft [10], and Apple [11]. Multidimensional data inherently contain inter-dependencies [12], [4], making privacy preservation challenging because correlation may reveal more information [13], [14]. Studies have proposed various methods to apply LDP in multidimensional data—e.g., privacy budget splitting [15], random-sampling [16], joint perturbation [4], etc.—to overcome correlation-induced privacy leakage (CPL) impact. These approaches primarily assume that the prior knowledge of the attribute dependencies is unknown/unavailable. However, studies highlight that prior knowledge of such correlations is ubiquitous today; for example, through universal correlation information [17], publicly available datasets [18], and learning distributions from a subset of the population [19]. Therefore, when prior knowledge is available, leveraging it to analyze CPL enables more nuanced applications, including evaluating the privacy guarantee and calibrating the privacy budgets of the mechanisms [20], [14], [18]. Pioneer work [21] uses conventional correlation metrics— e.g., mutual information (MI) [22], Pearson correlation coef-ficient (PCC) [23]—to estimate CPL. However, recent stud-ies [14], [20] claim that these conventional correlation metrics are unable to accurately characterize the CPL, even when ground-truth probability distributions between attributes are available. Recent studies propose algorithms to estimate CPL between attributes using probability distributions to address limitations of conventional correlation metrics. For instance, suppose we need to compute the CPL of an attribute Xk

caused by a correlated attribute ˆX. Let a and b be the alphabet sizes 1 of Xk and ˆX, respectively. Study [20] (ITS) and study [18] (CBP) quantify CPL only for special-case LDP mechanisms whose randomization can be parameterized by two perturbation-probability levels (e.g., k-RR [5], Unary en-coding [24]), which limits applicability to more general mech-anisms (e.g., staircase mechanism [25]). These algorithms have time complexities of O(ab ) and O(2 a), respectively. A recent study [14] proposes two algorithms for estimating CPL. The first algorithm, HCC-1, estimates the actual CPL using the transition probabilities of the LDP mechanism and the joint probability distribution between attributes. The second algo-rithm, HCC-2, estimates the supremum value of the CPL for any LDP mechanism, using a privacy budget of ε and a joint probability distribution between attributes. These algorithms

Problem Statement - In summary, although quantifying CPL is a promising solution to evaluate privacy guarantee and maximize privacy-utility trade-off in multidimensional data, existing metrics and algorithms have two key shortcomings 

that limit their practical usability on real-world data: 

• P1 - Limited to specific LDP mechanisms or scalability limitations. 

• P2 - Requires ground truth probability distributions between the attributes .Next, let us consider a motivational example.

Motivation Example - Suppose we conduct a state-wise household income survey in the United States under LDP. Data from an initial subset of states (e.g., five states) can be collected using correlation-agnostic baselines such as simple privacy–budget splitting (SPL) or random sampling [27]. As the survey progresses, we can estimate joint attribute depen-dencies from initially collected data and treat them as prior knowledge . This prior knowledge is then used to optimize attribute-wise privacy–budget allocation, thereby improving the privacy–utility trade-off (Figure 1). In this setting, dis-tribution shifts primarily arise from demographic differences 

such as development level. Then, multiple sources (previously collected state data) provide the basis for learning distributions and their potential uncertainty. However, currently available CPL analysis algorithms cannot utilize this distributional uncertainty information for privacy leakage analysis, which could lead to privacy vulnerabilities due to incorrect privacy leakage estimates. 

Proposed Solution - We first generalize the optimization problems studied in [14], [26] to settings where the underlying data distribution is uncertain (Section III). In this regime, computing the optimal CPL is substantially more expensive— with worst-case exponential-time complexity—thereby moti-vating heuristic methods to estimate CPL. We then ask whether the probability distribution can be compressed into a low-dimensional summary that preserves the dependency structure relevant to CPL, enabling fast evaluation. Conceptually, this approach mirrors the use of conventional correlation metrics, but seeks to leverage their computational and storage advan-tages while mitigating their accuracy gaps. Our main finding is that the pairwise dependency infor-mation relevant to CPL assessment (including distribution uncertainty) can be captured by three parameters . Using these 

dependency statistics —(α, β, δ )–Dependency Triad (DT) —we construct a constant-time estimator (using computed parame-ters α, β and δ) that provides a tight upper bound on CPL for any privacy budget ε. Here, the nonnegative coefficients α and 

β quantify complementary aspects of dependence: intuitively, 

α governs leakage behavior in the low privacy regime (larger 

ε), while β governs the high privacy regime (smaller ε). The factor δ ∈ [0 , 1) corrects for sparsity 2 by capturing mass concentration in the distribution (We provide an empirical interpretation of the DT parameters in Section VI-D). Given DT, Algorithm 2 estimates CPL in constant time O(1) . DT only occupies constant space O(1) , in contrast to storing the full a×b joint distribution at O(ab ) to keep dependency infor-mation. Therefore, constant time and space requirements ad-dress P1 . Experts can calibrate (α, β, δ ) from prior, potentially uncertain, distributional knowledge, enabling deployment in real-world LDP data collection and privacy analysis pipelines (addressing P2 ). Finally, we prove that the DT-based estimator is an upper bound on CPL, ensuring that reported leakage never understates the true privacy loss and thereby overcoming privacy vulnerabilities of existing methods.

Our Contributions - Next, we summarize our key contribu-tions in this paper as follows: 

• We show that the pairwise dependency information relevant to CPL assessment, including distributional uncertainty, can be summarised by three parameters. We propose this three-parameter representation, termed the 

Dependency Triad (DT), as a novel metric for modeling attribute dependencies in LDP privacy-leakage analysis. Rather than fully characterizing the underlying joint distribution, DT serves as a conservative surrogate for pairwise CPL, enabling tractable leakage assessment when the full distribution is unavailable or uncertain .We further use pairwise analysis as a practical building block to estimate total leakage in multidimensional data. To the best of our knowledge, DT is the first metric specifically designed to summarize dependencies for LDP privacy leakage analysis in this setting. 

• We theoretically prove that DT possesses several desir-able properties—configurable tolerance to uncertainty in prior distributions, constant-time complexity, and com-patibility with sparse probability distributions. 

• We validate the theoretical guarantees via extensive empirical evaluation on both synthetic and real datasets. Moreover, we demonstrate how to incorporate distri-butional uncertainties in privacy analysis and calibrate privacy budgets in real-world data collection scenarios, highlighting the potential practical applicability of DT.

C. Inaccuracies in Distributional Information 

In LDP deployments, a prior (learned or public) rarely matches the ground-truth distribution of the target population. Mismatch can arise from demographic differences , sampling bias , temporal drift or stale knowledge . When the prior does not reflect the actual dependencies among attributes, CPL computed from the prior can be shifted—either underestimated 

or overestimated .Let PA(∈ Ra×b) and PK (∈ Ra×b) be the actual and known conditional distribution between Xk and ˆX (i.e., P ( ˆX|Xk))respectively. Let CPL ⋆ denote the estimated CPL of the PA

and CPL ′ denote the estimated CPL of PK . We say CPL is underestimated if CPL ′ < CPL ⋆ (calibration may grant more budget than is safe), and overestimated if CPL ′ > CPL ⋆

(calibration is conservative, possibly reducing utility). Since the sign of misspecification is generally unknown, mitigating the underestimation risk guarantees the privacy. We formulate the distributional mismatch as an uncertainty set around the known distribution PK (∈ Ra×b). Let ∆( ∈

Ra×b) be the individual entry-wise difference between PA

and PK (i.e., ∆ ≥ | PA − PK |). This provides fine-grained variations for PA from PK . For example, we can define relative uncertainty with respect to the known distribution

B. Time Complexity Analysis 

This optimization problem is inherently non-convex, as the objective function involves a bilinear fractional form. Con-sequently, computing the optimal CPL becomes substantially more expensive than in the fixed (G, G ′) setting (i.e., without distributional uncertainty), where the problem reduces to a linear fractional program (LFP). A potential approach is to decompose the original problem into quasi-convex sub-problems by fixing the subset S and solving each sub-problem independently. The global optimum can then be obtained by taking the maximum across all 

S. For a fixed S, maximizing F (·) requires increasing the numerator term P

Similarly, if G and G′ are fixed, an optimal solution can be computed efficiently using standard methods for linear fractional programs (LFPs), yet exploring all possible combinations of their bounds still incurs 

O(2 b) complexity. Since no straightforward convex relaxation exists, the stan-dard approach to handle bilinear fractional programs (BFPs) is to linearise the bilinear terms—typically via McCormick relaxations or related techniques—yielding a mixed-integer linear program (MILP). While this transformation enables the use of off-the-shelf MILP solvers, it remains computationally expensive, exhibiting exponential complexity in the worst case [31]. 

C. Our Approach 

Heuristic approaches are usually adopted to solve these types of problems. In this study, we focus on extracting CPL-related information from distribution and utilize it to compute the CPL in time efficiently, similar to conventional correlation metrics, while mitigating the privacy risks associated with them. In this section, we experimentally evaluate and validate the proposed Dependency Triad (DT) metric. We first describe the experimental setup in Section VI-A. We then compare DT with competing metrics in Section VI-B. Section VI-C validates the CPL estimated by DT against CPL ⋆, while Section VI-E evaluates its robustness under distributional uncertainty. Sec-tion VI-F evaluates DT under different structural regimes, demonstrating that it is not limited to a single dependency pattern. Section VI-G shows how DT can be used to calibrate privacy budgets in data collection settings that incorporate prior knowledge. Section VI-H conducts attribute inference attacks on data collected under privacy budgets calibrated using DT. Finally, Section VI-I compares DT with random sampling. 

A. Experimental Setup 1) Environment: We implement our experiments using Python 3.10 and run them on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 3955WX processor, 128GB of RAM, and 4TB of storage. 

2) Datasets: We use five real datasets. In these datasets, we have converted continuous numerical attributes into discrete attributes by binning, as this work focuses on privacy leakages related to discrete/categorical data. 

SPM [28] dataset is a community survey conducted in the USA on the Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), which contains 38 attributes with more than 3M samples. 

CelebA [33] dataset contains 40 binary attributes of human faces with more than 200K samples. 

Adult [34] is an income survey dataset containing 14 features and over 40K samples. 

CVD [35] dataset consists of 11 features of 70K cardiovas-cular patient records. 

DSS [29] dataset is released by the Department of Social Services (DSS) in Australia, and the selected dataset contains 27 features, with more than 38K samples.

B. Benchmark 

We use state-of-the-art six benchmarks: IST [20], CBP [18], HCC-1 [14], HCC-2 [14], LTM [26] and MI [22]/PCC [23] to compare DT as summarized in Table II. Unlike existing approaches, DT is the first metric to show that three parameters can characterize the dependency to quantify the potential CPL under LDP. The polynomial-time precomputation of DT is a one-time offline calibration step. After calibration, CPL can be evaluated in constant time for any privacy budget. By contrast, baselines such as HCC-1, HCC-2, and LTM require repeated full computation for each candidate privacy bud-get. This distinction matters in practice, since privacy-budget calibration often requires evaluating many candidate privacy budgets to explore the privacy–utility trade-off, especially for large-cardinality attributes. Moreover, DT has addressed the long-lasting distribution uncertainty challenge in CPL calculation as DT is adjustable to distribution uncertainties and computationally efficient. Appendix L empirically validates the time complexity and space complexity of DT.

VII. R ELATED WORK 

We start this section by exploring the concept of prior knowledge and its implications for privacy risks. We then review existing CPL analysis algorithms under Differential privacy (DP) and Local differential privacy (LDP). 

A. CPL and Prior Knowledge 

Prior knowledge can manifest in various forms depending on the data context, ranging from statistical distributions [21], [17] to temporal dependencies in time-series data [2], [20], and spatial inferences such as estimating the current location from previously observed ones [20], [3]. Studies have shown that ad-versaries may acquire such knowledge through multiple chan-nels, including public datasets [34], [33], social norms [17], or even during the data collection process itself [19], [12]. These sources of prior knowledge significantly heighten privacy risks, and (L)DP mechanisms require explicitly accounting for such knowledge to safeguard privacy [17], [21], [12]. 

B. CPL Computation in DP and LDP 

DP [38] and LDP [8] have emerged as standard tech-niques for safeguarding privacy, primarily due to the limi-tations of earlier approaches such as k-anonymity [39] and 

l-diversity [40]. LDP is widely employed when the data collector is considered untrusted [3], [11], [10], [9]. Study [13] is a pioneering work that highlights the privacy risks with correlated data under the central setting of DP. Then, studies propose different methods to address the privacy risks in corre-lated data. Group DP is a simple method to adjust the global sensitivity based on the number of correlated records [41]. Privacy budget splitting and sampling methods are commonly adopted methods to apply LDP in correlated data [27]. These methods primarily assume no prior knowledge. When prior knowledge is available, studies propose different algorithms to measure the CPL using prior knowledge. We can group them into two main categories as discussed in the following sections. 

1) Conventional Correlation Metrics Based CPL Analysis and Their Problems: Early studies tend to adopt conventional correlation metrics (e.g., mutual information (MI), Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), and covariance) to measure the CPL. Study [21] formulates the sensitivity parameter using a correlation degree matrix. Another study [42] uses distance correlation to measure the correlation. In [43], a periodic sensi-tivity calculation algorithm is proposed to address the periodic correlation changes in temporal data using autocorrelation. Study [18] proposes a covariance-based algorithm to quantify CPL, and [44] calibrates privacy budgets using PCC to trade-off privacy and utility. However, these conventional metrics are unable to correctly characterize some critical properties of CPL in (L)DP—e.g., asymmetric behavior and identifying the worst-case scenario [14]. 

2) Bayesian Theorem-Based Algorithms to Compute CPL: 

Study [26] proposes an algorithm to quantify the CPL in DP. A recent study [20] has analyzed privacy leakage related to spatial-temporal data, a specific family of LDP mecha-nisms which have two levels of perturbation probabilities (i.e., GRR [30], Unary encoding [24], Hash encoding [24], etc.). Study [14] proposes two algorithms to compute CPL in any LDP mechanism. However, all these algorithms primarily assumed that ground truth probability distributions are known, which is usually not true, especially for locally perturbing scenarios like LDP. Furthermore, these algorithms have either polynomial or logarithmic time complexity over alphabet size, which can be challenging when CPL is iteratively evaluated at different privacy budgets to calibrate utility and privacy. In summary, privacy leakage analysis in correlated envi-ronments is crucial to guarantee the privacy of mechanisms as well as to maximize the utility by tuning the privacy budgets. However, the CPL analysis is currently performed using computationally expensive algorithms that assume the 

ground-truth distributions are known. Therefore, currently, there is no method to time efficiently estimate CPL under inaccurate distributional information .VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This work demonstrates that three parameters derived from the joint distribution of two attributes are sufficient to construct a conservative surrogate for pairwise correlation-induced pri-vacy leakage (CPL), even under distributional uncertainty. We formalise these parameters as a new metric, the Dependency Triad (DT), and provide a theoretical foundation for its use. DT is not intended to fully characterise the underlying joint distribution; rather, we prove that it yields a tight, uncertainty-aware upper bound on pairwise CPL. Further, we develop algorithms that enable practical CPL evaluation and privacy-budget calibration. Since exact multidimensional privacy leak-age analysis is often intractable, this pairwise formulation provides a tractable building block for assessing total privacy leakage across attributes. Collectively, these results show that DT is more than an empirical heuristic: the proofs certify when and why DT remains conservative enough to mitigate privacy risks, while staying tight in the nominal regime. Moreover, DT overcomes two key limitations of prior CPL computation methods by (i) explicitly incorporating configurable dependence through uncertainty in distributional information and (ii) enabling constant-time CPL estimation using the computed parameters of DT. Our empirical results corroborate the theory: DT accurately tracks CPL, particularly in high-privacy regimes (e.g., ε ≤ 1), and scales effectively to high-dimensional settings. 

Limitations and Future work - This work focuses on estimating CPL in pure LDP mechanisms using the proposed metric DT. We will extend this work to CPL estimation in approximated LDP mechanisms in our future work. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
